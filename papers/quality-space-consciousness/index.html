The whole paper in 5 minutes

Your mind may keep a map, not just a pile of feelings.

Imagine tasting a lemon, then a lime. They are different — but your brain also knows they are neighbours. This paper calls that neighbourhood map a quality space: a map of what experiences feel like, arranged by resemblance.

🟥 Red sits nearer to orange than green.
🎵 Two nearby notes feel more alike than a bass note and a high whistle.
🧠 The claim: these felt distances may mirror distances between brain-activity patterns.
Think Google Maps for feelings.Your current location is one feeling. The surrounding roads and places tell you where it is relative to the others. The paper asks whether the whole map helps make that location feel the way it does.

The clever measuring trick: compare patterns, not volume.

Researchers can show people lots of things and ask which seem alike. They can also record the brain’s response to those same things. If the two maps have the same shape, that is evidence that the brain pattern tracks the feeling.

1️⃣ People make a “what feels similar?” map.
2️⃣ Brain recordings make a “what activates similarly?” map.
3️⃣ If the maps line up, neural geometry may explain felt geometry.

That does not mean the loudest brain signal wins. A signal can fade while the pattern of relationships stays stable. The stable shape may be the important thing.

Two versions of the big idea.

Small claim: you experience relations among things happening now — red beside blue, a loud sound after a soft one. Big claim: even one lonely flash of red gets some of its feel from the entire set of colours your brain could represent.

Paper map or rubber sheet?Cut a corner off a paper map: your “you are here” dot stays put, but what the map represents has changed. Stretch a rubber sheet: the dot moves because the sheet deforms. The authors use this contrast to separate a deep, map-wide change from an ordinary change to the local signal.

Why consciousness theories care.

Some theories put conscious contents mainly in sensory brain areas. Others say information must be copied or broadcast to a wider brain network. All can handle the small claim. The big claim is harder for theories that need a full copy of the whole sensory map somewhere else.

📍 Local view: the sensory map itself can carry the feel.
🚪 Lean “gate” view: let the sensory map through, without redrawing all of it.
📋 Full-copy view: needs the map’s relationships copied too — a much taller order.

TL;DR 🧠💥

The paper says feelings may be like locations in a neural map. The key future experiment is brutal and beautiful: change the wider map, recreate the same local dot, and ask whether the experience changes. If it does, the map is doing more than holding the dot.

braingood · the full paper from first principles

The paper is about a very ordinary fact: experiences have neighbours.

When you see red, you do not merely have a private “red event.” Red seems closer to orange than to green. A high note seems closer to a nearby high note than to a low bass note. The authors ask whether this pattern of felt closeness is part of consciousness itself — and whether the brain carries a matching pattern.

Keep this one sentence

Maybe a feeling gets some of its character from its place in a map of other possible feelings.

The word “quality” here means what an experience is like. “Space” only means a set of locations defined by near and far relationships. It does not mean a physical place in the head.

1. Start: people can judge which experiences feel alike.
2. Look: the brain also has patterns that can be near or far.
3. Ask: is the pattern just a record, or part of what makes the feeling what it is?

The method without the buzzwords

Representational similarity analysis asks one simple question: do the two maps have the same shape?

For every pair of items, write down a distance. The left grid is “how different did it feel?” The right grid is “how different were the activity patterns?” The exact values can differ. What matters is whether the near pairs and far pairs line up.

Collect judgments

Use direct ratings, ask for the odd one out in a triad, let people arrange similar items near one another, or ask which reference item best matches a target.

Build an N × N matrix

Every cell records the distance between one pair among N tested items. The same format can hold subjective judgments, objective image features, or machine-learning model outputs.

Recover a space

Distances may be geometric or graph-like. Multidimensional scaling can make a low-dimensional picture of the relations. Perceived similarity need not follow physical distance; under Shepard’s scaling law it can fall exponentially with psychological distance.

The real disagreement

Does the map merely locate a feeling, or does the whole map help make the feeling?

Modest claim: active relations

When red and orange are both in your current experience, their relation can be felt. This is like seeing two dots at once and seeing the distance between them.

All the accounts discussed can handle this.

Stronger claim: whole structure

Even if only red is currently present, red’s particular feel partly depends on its relation to the whole available set of colors. The background map contributes to the current point.

This is the challenging, testable claim.

A test that could change minds

Hold the local pattern fixed. Change the surrounding map. Then see whether the experience changes.

The logic, slowly

Same dot, different map?

First alter the wider sensory activity space — for example, the connections that shape the map. Then recreate the original local pattern. If the experience changes anyway, the local pattern cannot be the entire story.

Step 1: red’s local pattern sits in its ordinary network of relations.

This is the paper’s central proposed experiment. It is hard: the strongest versions require precise causal interventions, most feasible in animal models, plus a way to assess perceptual similarity. That difficulty is why the paper is an agenda for future research, not a declaration that the case is closed.

Where theories enter — only now

Theories disagree about where the map must be represented.

Localist accountThe relevant sensory neural map itself helps realize experience. A map-wide effect is a natural possibility.
Lean workspace or higher-order accountA wider system can make a sensory representation conscious without copying every fine relation from the sensory map. It can also allow the sensory map to supply the quality structure.
Full-content workspace or higher-order accountIf the wider system must carry the conscious content in full, then a whole-map effect raises a demand: the wider representation must preserve the entire relational structure too.

Box 2 · what belongs in a quality space?

The proposal is broader, and more careful, than a color wheel.

Clear cases

Visual, auditory, and olfactory experiences can be arranged by subjective similarity. Color alone has separable dimensions such as hue, brightness, and saturation.

visionsoundsmellhuebrightnesssaturation

Possible extensions

Researchers can also build similarity spaces for facial identity, emotion, and the meaning of thoughts. The unresolved issue is whether those judgments reflect similarity in conscious character itself or only similarity in what is represented.

Separate spaces

Experiences need a shared dimension to occupy one space. Comparing two colors makes sense; comparing the color of one object with the size of another may not. People can still make cross-domain associations, such as sound–shape matches, without literally experiencing both in one quality space.

Method versus metaphysics

Structuralist methodology uses similarity structure to find neural realizers. Structuralism makes the stronger philosophical claim that relations to other experiences constitute what an experience is. The method can work even if the metaphysical thesis is false.

Localist account · the missing mechanism

A sensory population code can create a high-dimensional address for an experience.

“Localist” does not mean one neuron per feeling. It means the activity patterns that support conscious content are located primarily in sensory systems. Several neural populations are tuned to different attributes; their combined firing rates locate the current experience in a neural space.

Population code

Many tuned neurons form one coordinate

Different populations respond to motion direction, color, shape, or other attributes. Their joint pattern is a point in a high-dimensional perceptual space; distances between patterns can underpin psychological similarity.

Paper examplesfMRI, magnetoencephalography, intracranial recordings, and single-unit data can all be compared with RSA.
Face space

Faces as linear combinations

Macaque inferotemporal cortex represents individual faces as combinations of activity along a reported 50-dimensional “face space.” During binocular rivalry, perceived and suppressed faces were both decodable, but occupied distinct subspaces.

Why it mattersA stimulus can be neurally represented without having the same conscious status; the geometry may split conscious from suppressed content.
Time

Relations can be rich even under the modest view

A supposedly single experience varies slightly over time. The subject therefore encounters a family of nearby activity patterns and many actually instantiated relations, rather than one frozen dot.

PredictionDeleting an unused or “null” dimension should not change experiences that remain inside the restricted space.
Whole network

Lateral connections may realize the background map

On Malach’s proposal, local lateral connectivity in sensory cortex, input strength, feedback, and top-down modulation jointly determine the relational geometry. The wider network is actual neural machinery, even when the comparison experiences are merely possible.

Supporting cluesPosterior visual geometry can remain stable while response magnitude adapts; early layers of task-trained neural networks can show similar geometry.
Plasticity

Training can bend perceived space

Song and colleagues used Hebbian training to strengthen lateral links between two visual locations. Perceived distances between untrained locations also changed, suggesting that altering local connectivity can alter experienced spatial relations.

Important limitThis is suggestive, not the decisive fixed-pattern intervention: training may also deform the focal response.

Evidence audit · what supports what?

The paper assembles clues at different strengths; it does not treat them as equivalent.

Subjective and neural maps correspond

Perceived color, shape, function, background, and total similarity can correlate with representational geometry in different parts of ventral visual cortex. A cited 118-image study separates these dimensions.

Supports neural realization of similarity structure; does not choose a consciousness theory.

V4 tracks color quality space better than V1

Color can be decoded from both V1 and V4, but cited work finds that V4 activity reflects the experienced color geometry. Structuralist methodology can therefore discriminate candidate neural realizers.

Shows why geometry is more informative than simple decodability.

Posterior geometry is sustained; prefrontal content is more transient

During sustained viewing, posterior visual patterns preserve relative dissimilarities even as overall activity magnitude adapts. Prefrontal cortex can encode rich content, including during rapid serial visual presentation, but its content coding is more onset-linked and task-flexible.

Useful tension: PFC contains content, yet may read out or reformat sensory geometry rather than duplicate it faithfully.

Task demands reshape prefrontal spaces

Task-relevant dimensions in PFC can be compressed or stretched. That flexibility may count against PFC geometry fixing phenomenal character, although attention and task can also alter appearance, so the inference is not settled.

A live ambiguity, not a clean refutation.

Reality and magnitude signals have partial support

Near-threshold judgments combine signals from perception and imagination, consistent with reality monitoring. But PFC and parietal regions also show rich content, and medial PFC prediction errors about perceptual magnitude can encode content features.

Supports lean gating signals while challenging an account where those regions carry only content-free tags.

Abstract grid codes may navigate perceptual spaces

PFC and hippocampus show grid- or place-like codes while navigating learned odor and auditory spaces. These may implement higher-order access to relations, but they could reflect the explicit navigation task rather than conscious quality.

Suggestive for perceptual reality monitoring; connection to phenomenal character remains unproven.

Outstanding questions from the paper

The review ends with a research program, not a winner.

Conscious versus non-conscious geometryDo unconsciously represented properties also form similarity spaces? If so, is there a geometry characteristic of consciousness?
Relations across timeDo instantiated relations only connect simultaneous experiences, or also experiences occurring within a nearby temporal window?
Stability and gatingCan correlated stability among individually noisy sensory features make them jointly conscious, as lean higher-order theories predict?
Beyond visual adaptationCan behavioral training alter local connectivity and perceived quality spaces in sound, smell, and other modalities?
A neural signature of an indexWhat would a higher-order index and its own similarity space look like in neural data?
PRM versus HOSSMust relations be rebuilt among higher-order indices, or is it enough to tag the first-order sensory geometry as real?
Distributed time and spaceHow does a quality space change over time when its neural similarity structure is distributed across regions and moments?
Simultaneous featuresHow does the brain encode relations among features that appear together in one experience?

The paper in one honest paragraph

Fleming and Shea are not saying that scientists have solved consciousness or found a literal color map in the brain. They review evidence that the shape of neural activity patterns can mirror the shape of felt similarity. The modest claim — that we experience relations among currently active contents — fits all the theories they examine. The stronger claim — that the whole sensory geometry helps constitute each experience — fits localist and lean gating/sharing accounts more naturally than full-content accounts, which must reproduce the whole structure. The decisive evidence would come from interventions that change local or global geometry while controlling the focal pattern.
